## 1. Purpose and scope

The design tool lets a user plan a smaller plant, or switch parts of it off. The plant is designed so that every material and heat stream is used again somewhere: scrap is remelted, chlorine goes back to the leach, waste heat is stored and reused. When a part is missing, the streams it used to take in have nowhere to go, and the streams it used to send out stop arriving. This page lists those streams so the design tool reports them as unreclaimed resources, open problems to solve, instead of treating the plant as still closed.

Two questions are answered here:

1. If a sector is switched off, which streams are left unreclaimed? (Section 3.)
2. If the plant is built smaller, how small can each sector go before it stops working, and what does a smaller size cost? (Section 4.)

Every list on this page is computed from the plant graph by a balance on each sector, and the size limits of section 4 come from the mass and energy balances of the sectors concerned.

## 3. Switching a sector off

### 3.1 Method

Draw a boundary round the sector and list every stream that crosses it (a mass and energy balance on a control volume). When the sector is off, each of those streams loses one end:

- A stream coming in from another sector loses its sink. The material or heat is left unreclaimed in the sector that makes it.
- A stream going out to another sector loses its source. That sector goes short of something it needs.
- Electrical feeds, including electric heaters that top up a heat store, and purchased imports stop. Exports and vents through the dock stop with the sector. Neither is a closure gap.
- A stream whose rate is zero in the current design carries nothing today. It is listed for completeness only.

The generator recomputes these lists from the plant graph on every regeneration, so they follow design changes. The design tool reads the same data (`design_tool/aqs_design_tool/data/scale_down.json`).

## 4. Building the plant smaller

### 4.1 Method

Throughput is taken as proportional to the scale factor s (assumption A-M-329; AQS-SCALE-001 gives the baseline feed of 150 kg/d at s = 1). Equipment does not shrink smoothly: a crucible, an electrolysis cell or a heat store has a smallest practical size, and some losses do not fall with throughput. For each unit type the question is therefore what stops working, or what gets worse, as s falls. Batch and campaign operation are the usual answer at small production rates [2]. Values used below come from the plant graph; values not yet confirmed by test are in the assumptions register with the test that will confirm them.

### 4.2 HEAT: heat stores

A heat store recovers waste heat from other sectors and gives it back later. Its recovered heat in is the waste heat it receives from other sectors plus the heat passed down from the store one tier hotter. Exchanges between the A and B twins of a tier cancel and are not counted, and purchased electric trim heat is not recovered heat. Its standing loss depends on its surface area, insulation and temperature, not on how much heat passes through it [3, 4]. If the store keeps its size while the plant shrinks, the recovered heat falls in proportion to s but the loss does not. The store stops delivering any useful heat when

    s x (recovered heat in) = standing loss,   that is   s_min = standing loss / recovered heat in.

Where an input is not yet rated it is counted as zero, so the limit shown is on the safe (high) side. Below s_min the store needs purchased heat just to stay warm, so it should be switched off and its recovered heat inputs are unreclaimed. If the store is instead made smaller with the plant, its loss falls with its surface area (volume to the power 2/3) and the limit drops to (standing loss / recovered heat in) cubed. Table 4.1 gives both. The design tool applies the first, more cautious limit.

### 4.3 FOUNDRY: metal production

Molten-salt electrolysis cells need their bath kept molten. The heat to do that (hold power) is set by the cell size and insulation, while the electrolysis power scales with metal output [5]. Letting a cell freeze between runs and remelting it cycles the lining through large temperature changes, which cracks refractory [6], so the cells are held hot (assumption A-M-330). At scale s the energy per kilogram of metal rises by the factor (s x D + H) / (s x (D + H)), where D is electrolysis power and H is hold power. Table 4.2 gives that factor. A cell whose hold power is already most of its total is a candidate for campaign operation with a shared hot-holding furnace; that is a design decision, not an automatic consequence.

### 4.4 POLYMERS: plastics

The polymer train makes grams to a few hundred grams a day, far below the smallest continuous equipment, so it already runs in campaigns of one polymerization kettle batch [1, 2].
